\begin{abstract}
This draft describes a two-rover prototype for cooperative exploration of a
lunar test field. Rovers detect visual scientific markers, share locations,
allocate inspections, recover unfinished work after a rover becomes unavailable,
and return to their bases. A nearest-rover policy and an energy-aware heuristic
share the same battery feasibility constraints. The implementation includes
typed ROS 2 interfaces, a generated Gazebo world, camera-based marker perception,
mission telemetry, and a portable rehearsal of the coordination logic.
Comparative Gazebo experiments remain pending. This document presents the design
and protocol without claiming measured policy improvements.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
multi-robot systems, lunar exploration, task allocation, ROS 2, Gazebo, energy
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Cooperative exploration requires both distributed observations and decisions
about which robot should execute each task. Multi-robot task allocation provides
a framework for separating missions, tasks and robot capabilities \cite{gerkey}.
Here, two rovers search separate regions and exchange detections. An inspection
can be assigned to a rover other than the observer when travel cost and energy
availability favour that decision.

The research question is whether including remaining energy in allocation
improves target completion and safe return compared with a distance-only policy.
The contribution is an integration and an experimental comparison of explicit
heuristics, not a claim of a new optimal allocator or flight-ready lunar autonomy.

\section{Related Software and Scope}
The Space ROS lunar pole exploration demo provides a relevant reference with
a VIPER-inspired rover, Mons Mouton terrain, cameras, solar-generation plugins
and a rechargeable battery \cite{spacedemos,spacesim}. These sources are recorded
at fixed revisions. The baseline uses lightweight models. An experimental adapter
prepares the upstream rover geometry and steerable joints with namespaced Gazebo
controllers. Physical validation, detailed terrain and solar integration remain
pending.

The target stack is ROS 2 Jazzy and Gazebo Harmonic, connected through
\texttt{ros\_gz}. OpenCV performs colour segmentation. Ground-truth odometry
removes localisation uncertainty from the first comparison. The current design
cannot support conclusions about SLAM, arbitrary rough terrain, real mineral
identification, or physical solar energy management.

\section{Mission and Architecture}
The mission comprises deployment, sector exploration, visual discovery, allocated
inspection and return to base. Uniquely coloured spheres represent five points
of scientific interest. The rovers have different routes and initial battery
fractions. Success requires every target to be confirmed and both rovers parked
at their respective bases.

\subsection{ROS Components}
Each rover has a custom agent and camera detector. A central coordinator receives
shared detections and status, publishes leased inspection goals and records
telemetry. The agent produces velocity commands, tracks its battery, confirms
nearby targets from local camera observations and handles returning and charging.
A monitoring node publishes RViz markers and paths. A local mission console
shows camera feeds, routes and events, with pause, resume and return controls.

Typed messages separate observations, resource state, ownership and completion.
Sensor and command topics are namespaced; mission topics are shared. All mission
coordinates use the world frame.

\subsection{Visual Perception}
HSV masks and contour filters detect five marker colours. Small, clipped or
strongly non-circular blobs are rejected. For marker diameter $D$, focal length
$f_x$, observed width $w$, horizontal centre $u$, and principal point $c_x$,
the planar estimate is
\begin{equation}
z_c \approx \frac{f_xD}{w}, \qquad
y_c \approx -\frac{(u-c_x)z_c}{f_x}.
\end{equation}
The camera offset and closest timestamped rover pose transform the estimate
into world coordinates. This approximation assumes a mostly level field.
Detection error must be measured separately against simulator truth.

An inspection requires the assigned rover to reach its stand-off, face the
marker and receive recent local observations over a dwell period. A teammate's
report alone cannot confirm a task.

\subsection{Control}
Agents follow sector waypoints and sweep their cameras at survey stops. The
80 by 60 m scene includes mapped rocks, outpost structures and crater exclusions.
Bounded planar A* inflates these hazards, shared marker estimates and peer poses
by the rover footprint and a safety margin. A forward laser scan also detects
unlabelled obstacles. A command gate checks curved motion and braking distance;
missing range data stops motion. Opposing rovers commit to passing on the right.
Inspections use fixed stand-off approaches and recent local camera observations.
These safeguards target the supplied level scene; rough-terrain traversability
and general dynamic-obstacle navigation still require further work.

\section{Allocation and Energy}
Each target has at most one owner, and each rover receives at most one inspection
at a time. Unique target IDs suppress duplicates. Leases distinguish current
assignments from expired work, and every reassignment receives a new task ID.

Let $d_{ij}$ be rover $i$'s approximate distance to target $j$'s stand-off,
$h_{ij}$ its estimated return distance, $b_i$ its battery fraction and $r$ a
reserve. The synthetic energy estimate is
\begin{equation}
e_{ij}=k_d(d_{ij}+h_{ij})+
k_t\left(\frac{d_{ij}+h_{ij}}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}+t_s\right).
\end{equation}
Both policies require $b_i \geq e_{ij}+r$. The baseline minimises $d_{ij}$.
The energy-aware policy minimises
\begin{equation}
J_{ij}=d_{ij}+\lambda\frac{e_{ij}+r}{\max(b_i,\epsilon)}.
\end{equation}
The recorded weight $\lambda$ has distance units. This greedy heuristic does not
solve global scheduling and may increase distance.

Energy decreases with measured distance and simulation time. Recharging at a
base is accelerated for practical demonstrations. These fractions are not
calibrated to a real rover. A* detours may exceed the straight-line estimate, so
the agent also checks its own return budget and may release work.

\subsection{Recovery}
A missing heartbeat, unavailable or returning rover, or task timeout releases
ownership. Failed tasks enter a cooldown and have bounded attempts. Results for
an expired task ID are ignored. A service disables and restores a rover for
repeatable failure interventions.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
Run matched experiments with the same world, initial state, camera settings,
mission duration, resource parameters and failure schedule. Only the policy
changes. Scenarios should include nominal exploration, asymmetric batteries,
temporary rover failure, and the combined energy/failure case.

Retain failed and timed-out runs. Report completion time alongside success rate
instead of averaging only successful runs. Use distinct predefined layouts;
record and pair random seeds if noise or randomisation is introduced. Retain
software revisions, configuration, CSV telemetry, JSONL events and hardware
details with each run.

\subsection{Validation Boundary}
Portable tests check ownership, feasibility, leases, recovery, detours and
synthetic marker images. An offline rehearsal imports the same mission logic,
but uses kinematic motion and oracle detections. It does not validate wheel
contact, rendering, camera-based world positions or physical lunar operation.
A Linux gate separately checks rendered cameras, laser scans, odometry,
detections, assignments and conservative footprint separation during a compact
mission. Geometric clearance is not a measurement of physical contact forces.

\section{Results: Pending Gazebo Experiments}
\textbf{The policy comparison remains pending.} Report hardware and software
versions, paired-run counts, success rates, the metrics in Table~\ref{tab:metrics},
and a recovery timeline from actual Gazebo runs. Offline rehearsal values are
debugging evidence only. Equal performance or an energy/time trade-off is a
valid finding.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Ground-truth localisation, known-size coloured markers, planar control and an
accelerated battery limit external validity. Report sensitivity to the heuristic
weight and error in the return-budget estimate. The central coordinator is a
single point of failure. Further work includes physical validation of the detailed
rover adapter, uneven terrain, solar charging, terrain-aware navigation and richer
perception.

\section{Conclusion}
A prototype and comparison protocol have been prepared for cooperative lunar
exploration. Conclusions about the allocation policies remain pending actual
Gazebo comparisons.
