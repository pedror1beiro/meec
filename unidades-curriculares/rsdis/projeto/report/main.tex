\begin{abstract}
This work integrates two simulated rovers, visual perception, energy management
and recoverable task allocation for cooperative lunar exploration. ROS 2 Jazzy
nodes communicate with Gazebo Harmonic through typed bridges. Adapted Space ROS
plugins provide rechargeable batteries and solar generation with rendered
occlusion, while a mission gateway introduces repeatable communication faults.
Rovers share camera discoveries, inspect allocated sites and return to separate
bases. A nearest-rover baseline and an energy-aware heuristic use the same
feasibility and navigation rules. Actual Gazebo validation completes a compact
mission and an 80 by 60 m obstacle-rich failure scenario, with five confirmations,
both rovers returned and no conservative footprint overlaps. A frozen paired
experiment separates policy effects from layout and fault changes. The system
demonstrates package integration and observable failure recovery; its accelerated
power model, known terrain and ground-truth localisation constrain conclusions
about real lunar operation.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
multi-robot systems, ROS 2, Gazebo, task allocation, energy, fault recovery

\section{Introduction}
Exploration requires more than sending independent robots to waypoints. A rover
may discover a site that another can inspect with less travel or greater
remaining energy. A temporary failure can leave an assignment unfinished.
The team needs shared observations, explicit ownership, resource-aware
decisions and a defined completion condition. Multi-robot task allocation offers
a framework for distinguishing task requirements and robot resources
\cite{gerkey}.

Our RSDIS project investigates these concerns in a lunar-inspired mission.
Two rovers survey different sectors, discover five visual markers, exchange their
estimated locations and allocate inspections. Success requires all five
sites to be confirmed and both vehicles to return to their own bases. The
research question is whether adding battery risk to a greedy distance score
changes completion, travel and energy use under identical safety constraints.

The engineering contribution is the integration of existing simulation,
middleware and perception facilities into a reproducible cooperative system.
The group develops the mission agents, task protocol, perception pipeline,
navigation safeguards, power governor, communication gateway and observers.
Existing models, plugins and standard algorithms are credited explicitly.
We do not claim a novel optimal allocator or flight-qualified lunar autonomy.

\section{Reused Components and Integration}
The runtime targets Ubuntu 24.04, ROS 2 Jazzy and Gazebo Harmonic. Stock Gazebo
systems provide physics, differential drive, joint control, cameras, lidar and
ground-truth odometry. The installed \texttt{ros\_gz} bridge translates sensor,
command and power messages between Gazebo Transport and ROS 2 \cite{bridge}.
OpenCV supplies image conversion, HSV segmentation and contour operations
\cite{opencv}. These dependencies provide infrastructure rather than the
cooperative mission itself.

Space ROS supplies a lunar exploration demonstration and simulation repository
\cite{spacedemos,spacesim}. We pin these at revisions
\texttt{4b4eb8447423} and \texttt{13226b067d92}, respectively. The default world
uses lightweight rover geometry for practical software-rendered experiments.
An optional adapter retains the upstream VIPER-inspired meshes, inertias and
steerable joints, with namespaced controllers and steering-feedback interlocks.
Upstream terrain assets are available separately; the evaluated outpost is
our generated planar scene.

The energy package adapts five Space ROS C++ source/header files implementing
rechargeable batteries and solar panels. Changes include per-rover power
interfaces, held inputs with watchdogs, ROS battery units, exhaustion recovery
and rendering-thread handling. Original Apache-2.0 notices remain in the files.
A manifest records immutable upstream URLs and original/adapted SHA256 hashes;
an automated check verifies attribution. The source guide distinguishes copied
code, installed dependencies and group-authored logic.

\section{Mission Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the runtime boundaries. Each rover has an
agent, camera detector and power governor. A shared coordinator allocates
inspections. A gateway mediates mission traffic, while local sensors and motion
control remain available during a mission-link outage. Independent observers
collect geometry audits, electrical telemetry, camera error and outcomes.

\subsection{Interfaces and State Ownership}
Custom messages represent detections, rover status, leased goals, results and
authoritative mission state. Sensor and actuator topics use
\texttt{/rover1} and \texttt{/rover2} namespaces; shared topics use
\texttt{/mission}. All mission positions refer to a common world frame.
The coordinator alone decides whether a result belongs to the current owner
and task identifier. It publishes accepted results and a complete latched
snapshot, preventing a delayed obsolete result from making a site appear
confirmed in the console.

Local images, lidar, odometry and status use sensor-style best-effort delivery.
Goals, detections and results use reliable mission interfaces. Authoritative
state uses reliable, transient-local delivery with depth one, so a late observer
can reconstruct the current state. These are existing ROS 2 DDS QoS facilities
\cite{qos}; application leases provide a separate freshness contract.

The local web console shows actual RGB frames, observed sites, planned and
travelled routes, battery voltage/current and source power. Operators can pause,
resume, recall, disable a rover or disconnect its mission link. Recorded
checkpoints are labelled and their control buttons disabled. Pausing stops
mission commands while Gazebo and native battery physics continue.

\subsection{Discovery and Local Confirmation}
Five uniquely coloured spheres stand in for scientific sites. The detector
thresholds HSV masks and rejects small, clipped or strongly non-circular
contours. Known marker diameter and camera calibration give approximate range.
If $D$ is the diameter, $\alpha$ half the angular separation of the horizontal
silhouette edges and $\beta$ their mean bearing, the level-camera estimate is
\begin{equation}
z_c \simeq \frac{D\cos\beta}{2\sin\alpha},\qquad
y_c \simeq -\frac{D\sin\beta}{2\sin\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Camera extrinsics and the closest timestamped rover pose project the estimate
into world coordinates. Images without a pose within 0.2 s are discarded.
Unique colour IDs suppress duplicate target records; inconsistent position
updates are rejected. This is known-size marker ranging, not mineral recognition.

An inspection requires reaching a stand-off, facing the marker and receiving
recent local camera observations over a dwell interval. A teammate's report can
create an assignment but cannot alone confirm it. The detector does not load
target coordinates. A separate supervisor compares delivered estimates with
simulator truth solely for evaluation.

\section{Map and Motion Control}
The expanded outpost covers 80 by 60 m under lunar gravity. It contains 24 rocks,
four crater exclusions, a habitat and a solar array. Fourteen added rocks create
detours between survey stops without enclosing scientific sites or blocking
charging pads. All 20 prescribed survey legs remain connected for conservative
rover radii of 0.72 m and 1.35 m; 18 require deviations from the direct segment.
Figure~\ref{fig:map} depicts design geometry, not a recorded trajectory.
A 40 by 30 m compact field supports faster matched experiments.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/map.pdf}
\caption{Expanded outpost and illustrative planned survey routes. Darker rocks
are the added barriers. Paths illustrate A* detours; target locations are shown

\label{fig:map}

A bounded planar A* planner \cite{astar} inflates prior hazards by vehicle
footprint and safety margin. Camera-derived marker estimates and peer positions
also form exclusions. Continuous edge checks prevent grid connections through
obstacles. The agent aligns before translating and evaluates a sampled curved
sweep over reaction and braking time before issuing a command.

A front-mounted, 180-degree, 721-ray lidar operates at 10 Hz. Valid endpoints
provide eight seconds of local obstacle memory; peer returns do not leave static
trails. Missing or invalid scans stop motion. Facing rovers commit to right-side
passing points to avoid symmetric oscillation. Inspection approaches remain
fixed while the vehicle turns, avoiding pursuit of a shifting noisy estimate.
Survey stops perform camera sweeps to discover sites outside the forward view.

Known terrain and ground-truth pose isolate allocation from localisation
uncertainty. Craters are visual depressions with navigation exclusions rather
than a physical heightmap. The conservative circular footprint audit reports
geometric separation, not contact forces or traversability of lunar soil.

\section{Energy and Task Allocation}
\subsection{Native Simulator Energy}
Gazebo supports battery state and consumer power as simulation entities
\cite{battery}. Our adapted rechargeable plugin receives electrical load from a
ROS governor using measured odometry:
\begin{equation}
P_L=10+100\min(|v|,1)+15\min(|\omega|,2)\quad\mathrm{W}.
\end{equation}
The model uses 24 V nominal voltage and 0.25 Ah capacity. Solar supply is
$P_S=12\max(0,\mathbf n\cdot(-\mathbf s))$ W when an outward ray from the panel
towards the directional sun is unoccluded, and zero otherwise. Scene updates
perform the query in the rendering thread. A stationary rover within 0.3 m of
its own pad may receive 550 W dock power in an appropriate charging state.
These accelerated parameters make charging observable during a short
demonstration; they are not calibrated VIPER specifications.

Ignoring clipping and transient smoothing, charge evolves as
\begin{equation}
\dot q \simeq \frac{P_S+P_D-P_L}{3600 V},\qquad b=q/C.
\end{equation}
The plugin bounds $q$ within $[0,C]$, publishes at 10 Hz simulation time and
expires a held source after one second without updates. ROS BatteryState uses
amperes, ampere-hours, a fraction in $[0,1]$ and negative discharge current
\cite{batterystate}. The bridge copies these fields directly; adapting the
upstream percentage and current convention is therefore necessary.
Stale battery data makes the agent stop and become unavailable rather than
silently switching models. The portable rehearsal retains an explicitly
labelled synthetic approximation.

\subsection{Shared Feasibility and Different Scores}
The coordinator stores at most one owner per target and one active inspection
per rover. Let $d_{ij}$ be approximate outward distance to the stand-off,
$h_{ij}$ estimated return distance, $b_i$ reported battery fraction and
$r=0.15$ reserve. Its simple prediction is
\begin{equation}
e_{ij}=k_d(d_{ij}+h_{ij})+
k_t\left((d_{ij}+h_{ij})/v_{\rm ref}+10\right).
\end{equation}
Both policies reject a candidate if $b_i<e_{ij}+r$. The nearest-rover baseline
minimises $d_{ij}$; the energy-aware heuristic minimises
\begin{equation}
J_{ij}=d_{ij}+\lambda\frac{e_{ij}+r}{\max(b_i,0.01)}.
\end{equation}
Parameters are $k_d=0.006$ per metre, $k_t=0.0001$ per second,
$v_{\rm ref}=0.3$ m/s and $\lambda=8$ m. Target-ID order and rover-ID tie-breaking
make the greedy decision reproducible. The predictor is a resource-budget
heuristic, distinct from the measured electrical model. It is not fitted online
and cannot account exactly for A* detours, rotations or solar occlusion.
Agents also check their return budget and can release work for recharging.

\subsection{Recovery and Communication Faults}
Goals carry task IDs and two-second renewable leases. Unavailable or returning
rovers, stale heartbeats and task timeouts release ownership. Retried targets
receive a new task ID after a cooldown, with at most three attempts.
Duplicates and results for old assignments are ignored. Lease expiry is checked
against the original timestamp, so delayed delivery cannot extend an obsolete
task. A late heartbeat cannot replace a newer pose.

The gateway supports ideal delivery and a seeded degraded profile: 15\% loss
per attempt, 0.12 s delay and uniform $\pm0.05$ s jitter. A rover-1 blackout from
world time 20 to 32 s exercises loss of mission connectivity. Reliable
application traffic retries every 0.2 s until expiry; best-effort heartbeats are
not accumulated for later delivery. Goal renewals are coalesced and the queue
is bounded at 512 entries. Independent random streams per rover and channel
avoid coupling unrelated traffic through one generator.

This is an application-channel fault emulator above ROS, not a radio propagation
model or measurement of DDS packet loss. Local sensing remains active, and
operator pause, recall and final-stop channels are out-of-band. The experiment
therefore evaluates selected mission-link faults, not a complete partition.

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Reproducible Design and Measurements}
The frozen comparison contains nominal, asymmetric-energy, temporary-failure
and communication-outage scenarios. Two seeds perturb target $x,y$ coordinates
by at most 0.2 m before collecting outcomes. Each configuration is executed
under both policies, giving eight matched layouts and 16 Gazebo runs.
Nominal batteries start at 0.95 and 0.55; the asymmetric case uses 0.30 and 0.95.
Failure disables rover 1 at fixed world time 30 s for 30 s under both policies.
The separate expanded-map functional gate can trigger failure on an active task;
it is not pooled with matched comparisons. Each run has a 900 s simulated
deadline and uses native energy.

Observers retain success including return, confirmations, elapsed simulation
time, odometry distance, minimum geometric gap and overlap samples. Electrical
discharge is integrated as $\int\max(0,-VI)dt/3600$ Wh over the mission window;
solar and dock inputs are recorded separately so charging cannot cancel prior
discharge. Gateway counters record attempts, losses, retries, expiries and
delivery delay. Camera-location error is evaluated against truth outside the
control loop. Failed runs remain in the dataset.

Each run stores configuration, CSV telemetry, JSONL events, source fingerprint,
package versions, intervention service outcomes and termination reason.
The recorder combines genuine Gazebo overhead/RGB images with ROS telemetry
and can save a ROS bag. It does not generate a mission from oracle detections.
Software rendering makes wall time host-dependent; comparisons use simulation
time. With only two seeds per scenario, results are descriptive rather than
evidence of statistical superiority.

\subsection{Functional Validation}
Revision \texttt{6351d85} passes Jazzy/Harmonic integration, including physical
rotation in both directions, translation, lidar, actual cameras and task
messages. The compact mission finishes in 235.0 simulated seconds. The expanded
obstacle/failure mission confirms all five sites and returns both rovers in
746.5 s, travelling 423.4 m with minimum conservative obstacle clearance
0.2824 m and zero overlap samples. Both failure service calls succeed. Red-site
ownership transfers from rover 1 to rover 2, which confirms it; this is actual
cross-rover recovery rather than an offline rehearsal.

The native energy bench verifies exposed-panel production, rendered shade,
discharge, dock recharge and ROS units. Figure~\ref{fig:power} shows recorded
electrical telemetry. The exposed panel supplies approximately 7.17 W under the
configured sun incidence; the covered panel supplies zero. Historical detailed
rover checks validate its compact motion and camera mission with synthetic
energy, but do not establish native-energy or expanded-terrain performance.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The system makes the relationship between resources, observations and task
ownership inspectable. Shared discovery permits one rover to inspect another's
observation. Freshness checks and accepted state address the distinction between
receiving a message and accepting a current result. The power bench tests the
bridge independently of allocation, and the expanded map tests navigation and
recovery independently of the compact policy comparison.

Several limits remain. Ground-truth pose removes localisation drift, the prior
map contains terrain exclusions, and coloured markers simplify perception.
Planar control and circular audits do not evaluate soil mechanics, slopes or
contact forces. Battery coefficients, capacity and dock power are demonstration
parameters; the fraction-based predictor is not a physical route-energy
optimiser. The central coordinator is a single point of failure. Seeded
channel faults do not replace hardware network measurements. Two small layout
perturbations cannot establish generalisation across lunar terrain or prove
that either allocator is globally better.

Useful extensions are measured route-energy prediction including detours,
more independent layouts, onboard localisation, uneven terrain and distributed
coordination. Each should preserve the separation between observer truth,
operational measurements and declared assumptions.

\section{Conclusion}
The project combines existing Space ROS plugins, Gazebo systems, ROS 2 QoS and
OpenCV with custom cooperative mission logic. Actual simulation demonstrates
camera-based discovery and confirmation, energy telemetry, charging, safe detours
and reassignment after rover failure. Retained configurations and measurements
make the implementation reviewable and policy comparison repeatable.
Conclusions concern this controlled simulation setting and its explicit models.
